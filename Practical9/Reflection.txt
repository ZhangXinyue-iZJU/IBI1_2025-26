Reflection on Practical 9: Modelling Infections

This practical helped me understand how stochastic SIR models work to simulate disease spread. The basic SIR model was straightforward to implement, but the vaccination extension taught me how herd immunity slows transmission. The spatial model was more challenging, especially handling neighbor infections and recovery without overwriting data. I found the color mapping for the spatial heatmaps interesting, as it visualized the spread intuitively. The most important thing I learned is that probabilistic models show different outcomes each run, which reflects real-world disease uncertainty.
This reflection addresses the key questions about my experience with Practical 9: Modelling Infections. Overall, parts of the practical were easy, while others were challenging. Implementing the basic SIR model in SIR.py was straightforward, as the core logic of dividing the population into S, I, and R compartments translated easily into code. However, the spatial SIR model in spatial_SIR.py was hard—handling neighbor infections without overwriting data and defining grid bounds required careful problem-solving.
I enjoyed the troubleshooting process most, especially fixing errors in the vaccination and spatial models to get accurate results; the least enjoyable part was struggling with matplotlib heatmap labeling for the spatial model.
The most important thing I learned is the link between theoretical SIR models and real-world applications—seeing how parameters like vaccination rate affect disease spread helped me understand public health strategies, and I also learned the value of careful parameter checking and code documentation for model consistency.
